TVP's read on what's happening in Texas civic life, and what it means for young Texans.

Nov. 8, 2026 · Sophia DeLoretto-Chudy

After the Wave: TVP on Nov. 3 and the Work Ahead

Nov. 3 was not the finish line

On Tuesday night, Texas did something it hasn't done in a generation. Voters sent a Democrat to the U.S. Senate for the first time since 1988 and elected Gina Hinojosa, the state's first Latina governor and its first Democratic governor since Ann Richards. Every statewide race reported so far broke the same way, and the Texas House looks set to change hands for the first time in 24 years.

Those wins belong to the people who made them. They belong to Texans who waited in long lines after work, to first-time voters, to poll workers, and to organizers across the state who spent years registering, educating and turning out their neighbors.

But TVP isn't about winning elections. We exist to help young Texans build a civic life that lasts long after the yard signs come down.

Results are unofficial until the state certifies them in early December.

What Texans said on Nov. 3

This election was a verdict on how Texans have been governed. Voters were stretched by grocery bills, gas prices, insurance costs and decisions in Austin and Washington that made daily life harder. Even leading Texas Republicans pointed to tariffs, prices and the war with Iran when explaining what went wrong.

It was also a test of whether an election can be bought. The Senate race became the most expensive non-presidential race on record, and Republicans poured more than $280 million into ads, including more than $200 million from Elon Musk. Voters answered anyway.

They answered despite real obstacles:

  • More than 7,000 people were still in line across Austin an hour before polls closed.
  • At UT Arlington, where the county removed the campus polling site, students organized their own rides to the polls.
  • A state registration backlog meant some registered voters in Travis County had to cast provisional ballots.

And early voting broke the midterm record set in 2018.

Here's what the night did not mean: Texas did not become a blue state. The party of the people leading it changed. Democrats won a majority of the vote from an electorate where most voters don't call themselves Democrats. Among the people who voted, more had backed Trump in 2024 than Harris, and Republicans still held an edge in party identification. Texans moved. They didn't convert. We are a deeply purple state where people of every political stripe decided the people in charge had stopped working for them.

Why this moment is fragile

A wave that rises this fast can recede just as fast. Border counties that swung hard toward Republicans in 2024 swung back more than 38 points in two years. Voters who move that far that quickly are telling us they're open, and that their trust is still being earned.

Registration drives and get-out-the-vote programs matter, and this cycle proved it. But a registration or a door knock is tied to a date. Trust is what's built in the months in between. This was the first cycle with serious investment in the Rio Grande Valley, and most of it was built to end on Election Day. One Valley organizer credited reaching colonia residents who had never been asked about their issues before. That's a first conversation. A relationship is what comes from how they are engaged after.

Young voters show the same pattern. In exit polls, roughly three in four young Texans backed Talarico, two years after many young men swung the other way. Most young voters don't claim either party. Nationally, more than 80% said they want substantial change or a complete upheaval. They are judging results, and they don't owe anyone their loyalty. As one CIRCLE researcher put it, this is not a generation that will “acquiesce to transactional interactions.”

The risk hiding in the win

Most of the analysis since Tuesday has asked a single question: is this a realignment or a fluke? Both answers miss the danger in front of us. The voters who moved this year owe their loyalty to no one, many were never taught how their government works, and they are about to watch a divided one move slowly.

Young voters are the clearest case. In 2024, many were drawn to a candidate who promised sweeping change and seemed unbothered by the rules and procedures that slow government down. Two years later, many of the same voters chose the other party for much the same reason. They are coming of age in a job market and an economy that are not working for them, and they will reach for whoever offers a way out.

That makes this moment as risky as it is hopeful. The new governor can veto, appoint and call special sessions, but she cannot pass a law on her own, and the Texas Senate remains in Republican hands. Much of what voters hoped for, on abortion, guns, health care and schools, will require both chambers. If the people Texans just elected appear to be doing nothing, the voters who lifted them will turn away just as quickly, and they will walk away believing that participation changes nothing.

Few of them have been given the tools to see it any other way. In 2022, eighth-grade civics scores on the Nation's Report Card fell for the first time since the test began, and fewer than one in four students reached proficiency. In Texas, a 2021 law bars teachers from giving credit for students' political activism, including contacting their elected officials, and some districts pulled back on civics activities to stay clear of it. A generation that was never shown how power moves is now being asked to be patient with a government designed to move slowly.

Why TVP is built for this moment

The organizing that powered this cycle was built around a date. Registration drives and get-out-the-vote programs reached people who had never been asked, and that work mattered. But mobilization ends when the polls close, and the trust that keeps people engaged is built in the months and years that follow. Texas now has more mobilization infrastructure than it has ever had, and far less of the relational infrastructure that holds people in civic life between elections.

That is the work TVP was created to do. Political power comes from social capital: the relationships, networks and trust that make people show up for one another. Understanding that power comes from political knowledge. Most civic organizations build their own communities, through chapters, fellowships and newsletters, and teach the people already curious enough to sign up. TVP works the other way around. We go to the communities young Texans already belong to and bring civic life into them, or bring a civic community to the places they already love, like a film screening or a rec league game. That is how we build knowledge and social capital at the same time, and how we close the gaps traditional organizing leaves behind. Knowledge without belonging rarely sticks, and belonging without knowledge rarely becomes power.

We also know that information alone will not carry anyone through a slow legislative session. Learning how government works has to feel like something people want to be part of, in places where young Texans already gather, alongside people they already trust.

And there is a window now that did not exist before. Many of the leaders Texans just elected are new to their jobs, and they want to hear from the people they serve. For years, access to power in this state was something a donor class could buy. Everyday Texans now have a generational chance to earn that access through relationships, built long before anyone needs a vote or a favor, and TVP intends to help young Texans claim it.

Why we do this

None of this is about turning Texas into a swing state in 2028. Low trust, low turnout and the loneliness so many young people carry are symptoms of the same condition: a democracy at risk and a civil society that has stopped holding people together. The wave that arrived this month came before the levees were finished, and the work now is to build them with the water already high.

American democracy is flawed, and it always has been. But it is ours, and we get to decide how to build it, how to improve it, and how to use it to foster a healthy, happy society, with liberty and justice for all.
