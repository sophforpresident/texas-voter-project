Before The Ballot

How your ballot's levels of government actually work.

City & County

A county commissioner decides road maintenance, jail funding, and the county property tax rate. A city council member decides zoning, local ordinances, and the police and fire budget — closer to daily life than almost anything else on the ballot.

School District

A school board trustee approves the budget and bond measures, hires the superintendent, and sets attendance boundaries — not curriculum. Curriculum standards are set at the state level, by the State Board of Education.

State

A state legislator decides the state budget, school funding formulas, and most criminal law — the layer of government furthest from daily attention, closest to real power.

(The State Board of Education — the body that actually sets curriculum and adopts textbooks — is its own elected office most voter guides skip entirely.)

"

Adults under 25 are 20–30 percentage points less knowledgeable than older adults on basic questions — like which level of government handles what — a gap that explains roughly half of the turnout gap between young and older voters.

— Stockemer & Rocher, 2016, "Age, political knowledge and electoral turnout"

What ID Do I Need?

Texas asks for one of seven photo IDs. No photo ID? You can still vote with a supporting document and a short form. VoteRiders helps for free: 866-432-8683.

See accepted IDs → By Mail

Vote by Mail

In Texas, mail voting is limited — for example, voters 65 or older, voters with a disability, or voters away from their county. Check if you qualify and apply.

Mail voting rules → By Mail

Track Your Mail Ballot

Already applied or mailed it back? Follow your application and ballot with the state's tracker.

Problem at the Polls?

Call the nonpartisan Election Protection hotline: 866-OUR-VOTE (866-687-8683). Trained volunteers answer questions and take reports.

Every Voter Guide's Recipe

Pick-A-Candidate Cornbread

Serves: every voter guide in Texas  ·  Prep time: assumes you already know your way around the kitchen

Ingredients

  • 2–6 candidates, room temperature
  • Their stated positions on the issues
  • A dash of endorsements
  • A pinch of party affiliation

Instructions

  1. Compare the candidates.
  2. Pick the one whose ingredients match your taste.
  3. Season the cast-iron skillet — never explained
  4. Know why it has to be cast iron — never explained
  5. Vote.
The Missing Step

Every recipe like this assumes you already know how the kitchen works — how to register, where your polling place is, what a county commissioner actually controls versus a state legislator. That's not a footnote. It's the whole first half of the recipe, and most guides skip straight to step one.
